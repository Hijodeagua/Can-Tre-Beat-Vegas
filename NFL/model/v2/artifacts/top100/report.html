Top 100 talent: position, rank, and quarterbacks

2011–2025 · 4,096 games · every rate shown straight up and against the closing spread

Three questions: does any position group of Top 100 talent move games more than others, do higher-ranked players outperform lower-ranked ones, and how do games break down when a Top 100 quarterback faces one who isn’t listed.

Two numbers everywhere, because they answer different questions. SU asks “do better rosters win?” — obviously yes, and it pays nothing. ATS asks “does the market underprice them?” — the only version worth money, break-even 52.4%.

1. Does any position group weigh out?

Games split by whether the home team had more Top 100 players at that position than the visitor, or fewer. Blue is how far that advantage moves the result; orange is how far the closing spread has already moved to meet it.

Win-rate gap (pp) Spread swing (points)

The ordering is intuitive — quarterback dwarfs everything, then the lines and pass catchers — but the two bars move together. A Top 100 QB edge is worth 22.6 points of win rate, and the spread has already moved 7.2 points to account for it. Defensive line is the odd one out: more Top 100 defensive linemen goes with a slightly worse record, and the market barely moves for it either.

What survives the spread

Subtract the market and what is left is the cover-rate gap.

No position group weighs out. All eight intervals cross zero. Wide receiver is the largest at +3.4 pp and defensive line the most negative at -2.4 pp, but with ~800 games a side the standard error is ~2.5 pp. If you want the least-bad candidate for a real effect it is WR — worth watching, not worth acting on.

Rank weighting

The list is ordered, so a #3 should outweigh a #97. Three schemes tested: flat count, linear (101−rank)/100, and logarithmic 1/log₂(rank+1).

Rank weighting is a better description of reality — log-weighted talent correlates more strongly with final margin (0.214 vs 0.201). The catch is the middle column: it correlates more strongly with the closing spread too (0.508 vs 0.448). The books weight by rank as well, slightly better than we do, so against the number weighting makes things marginally worse.

Straight up the ordering is clean and monotone across the top three tiers: 68.4% → 60.4% → 53.8%, with only the bottom tier breaking rank. So yes — higher-ranked quarterbacks demonstrably win more.

Against the spread it flattens. The top-10 tier at 53.2% is the most interesting number here: it clears break-even, with P(ATS > 50%) = 0.95. But the interval [49.5, 56.8] contains both 52.4% and the coin flip, so on 729 games it is suggestive, not bankable.

Bucketed by gap it is not monotone, and the 0–10 bucket inverts — when the list separates two quarterbacks by fewer than ten places, the “better” one goes 47.0%. That is the honest read on list precision: the ordering carries real information in broad strokes and essentially none in fine ones. At 83–155 games a bucket, do not over-read any single row.

What this changes

Nothing in the model, and that is the finding. Position-split counts, rank-weighted talent scores and QB-tier flags all describe outcomes well and all get absorbed by the closing spread. The pattern repeats at every level of granularity: raw effect large and obvious, market-relative effect indistinguishable from zero.

Two threads worth keeping: wide receiver is the only position group whose leftover edge points meaningfully positive (+3.4 pp), and top-10 quarterbacks cover at 53.2%. Both sit inside their own confidence intervals today. Each season adds ~270 games; two or three more should settle whether either is real.
